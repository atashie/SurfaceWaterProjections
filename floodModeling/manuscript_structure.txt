<Background>
Recent literature on the modeling the areal extent of water on the earth's surface has tended to focus on translating remotely sensed data (optical from S2 or LandSat#; or SAR from S1) into flood masks, typically using machine learning algorithms.
This work is commonly applied to mapping flood extent (e.g., inundated landscapes) and water resources (e.g., areal extent of reservoirs).
Historically, actual predictive modeling of streamflow, inflows to reservoirs, or flood extent has been accomplished by physics-based models, but these are computationally complex, rely on data of uncertain quality or calibration that is not always possible, and tend to be less accurate than ML approaches.
Recently, work has been accomplished by Google Flood Hub and GloFAS to link streamflow forecasts to flood extent, but to our knowledge the quantification of the quality of these models tends to be limited the streamflow extremes, while inundated areal extent is generally derived by linking streamflow volumes to maps of water extent from flood mask derived from remote sensing and topography.
We believe that remotely sensed water masks are subject to high uncertainty, and thus it is more meaningful to have models infer hydrologically realistic outcomes rather than blindly optimizing an objective function.
Further, remotely sensed inundated areal extents are most uncertain when mapping shallow water or small water bodies.
</Background>

<Our General Approach>
We are developing a framework for physically-informed, empirical machine learning models for modeling inundated vs non-inundated landscapes.
The only inputs are global, freely available static geophysical data, and global, freely available weather information.
The utility of our models is in 1) predicting floods; 2) predicting droughts and general water supply (e.g., "the reservoir/river is likely to be dry").
Our work is not meant to generate a finalized model, but to instead explore and test a variety of ideas and arrive at a series of suggestions for future work. 
</Our General Approach>

<Specific Hypotheses we test>
H1 – Hydrologically constrained features vs. model complexity: For inundation prediction under substantial uncertainty in both inputs (e.g., DEMs, LULC, soils, infrastructure) and targets (inundation extent masks), constraining models to operate on hydrologically meaningful features and representations leads to more robust and accurate predictions than increasing the complexity of the underlying ML architecture alone.
H2 – Regional data biases and basin-scale training: Regional biases and blind spots in global geospatial datasets (e.g., DEM, LULC, soils, infrastructure) manifest as regional biases in globally uniform flood models, but can be mitigated by training, validating, and testing models at the basin scale within a consistent framework.
H3 – Generalization of empirical basin-scale models: A purely empirical inundation model trained at the basin scale can generalize to predict out-of-sample flood inundation—both spatially, in held-out watersheds, and temporally, for novel flood events—provided that it is supplied with sufficiently rich hydrometeorological and geomorphic context.
</Specific Hypotheses we test>

Figure 6: shows the relative performance of xgboost models after manipulation of the features used to force it
	XGB - All Features: the basic XGBoost model with all features; this is the baseline model for this experiment
	XGB - Basic Features: XGBoost model with only River Basin Streamflow, HAND, and elevation as input features; this is intended to mimic the feautres used in Google Flood Hub and GloFAS and other similar models that directly relate coincident river basin level streamflow to flood inundation (where flood inundation is a 1-1 relationship between river basin streamflow and likely inundation as based on remotely sensed flood maps, HAND, and / or elevation maps); this iteration underperforms the baseline model in 6 out of 7 categories (and performs "as well" in the 7th category), indicating that river basin streamflow, HAND, and elevation alone are insufficient to accurately delineate inundated landscapes
	XGB - No Streamflow: the basic XGBoost model with all features except River Basin streamflow or Subwatershed streamflow (i.e., the only time-varying features are weather features and time-lagged weather features); here we test whether or not streamflow is essential to estimate inundation extent or whether an overlying model is capable of inferring streamflow impacts when supplied well designed weather features; this model performs exactly as well as the baseline model in all categories, indicating that river basin streamflow and subwatershed streamflow are not necessary features for modeling inundated areal extent, if the models are otherwise provided with well engineered weather features
	XGB - Multi Objective: the basic XGBoost model but with all "raw" features being predicted by the aggregated features (i.e., local LULC, local elevation, coincident river basin streamflow, etc., are removed from the dataset, then independent XGBoost models are built to predict those "raw" feautres the other derived features, then all derived and estimated features are used to predict inundated landscapes; this test is mean to assess whether or not smoothing uncertainty in the underlying features has an impact on overall model performance; this experiment performs worse in 6 and better in 1 category
	XGB - 25% Noise: the basic XGBoost model with all features, but with 25% of all positives (pixels inundated by water) flipped to negative, and the same number of negatives flipped to positive. Here, we introduce more uncertainty into our training target to highlight the idea that while the actual training target (S1 derived inundation masks) are highly uncertain, an empirical model fed well engineered features is nonetheless able to make realistic hydrological inferences from the remaining signal.
